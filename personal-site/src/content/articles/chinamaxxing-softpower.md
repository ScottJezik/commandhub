## Projections on the Internet

Charlie Kirk, the prominent right-wing advocate, was publicly assassinated at Utah Valley University, just 3 miles from where I live. It only takes me 8 minutes to drive to that spot, and it took the internet only days to satirize a man's lifework after his public death by using AI to create deepfakes of him in movies, street interviews, or associating with other figures that have cult followings, such as [George Droyd](https://knowyourmeme.com/sensitive/memes/people/george-droyd).

The internet is a location for nations, global actors, and interest groups to both covertly and overtly argue for their cause. Examining viral trends reveals what society is thinking and what powers are persuading individuals to think.

## The Internet as Civil Society

These humorous reactions to a public killing may have been unconscionable a few years ago. Internet discourse and humor have become increasingly desensitized and politicized for everyday users, now commonly covering issues such as pedophilia and conspiracy through the case of Jeffrey Epstein, making light of the events unfolding in Palestine with jokes like ["The Big Yahu"](https://blogs.timesofisrael.com/save-me-big-yahu-a-tiktok-trend-to-be-noticed/), and public assassinations with Charlie Kirk. This is all taken humorously.

Social media trends, while satire, are becoming a vital part of people's news sources and reflect both what people are thinking and the agendas nations push. Indeed, the Trump administration has used [social media](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-the-presidents-twitter-account-affects-civil-society/) since President Trump's first term as a primary form of communication and public forum. This has become a trend on the world stage, with Iran using [AI-generated videos](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cjd8jrd1vnyo) of Lego figures as propaganda targeted to Western audiences.

## ChinaMaxxing Analysis

The [ChinaMaxxing trend](https://chinaglobalsouth.com/podcasts/the-chinamaxxing-trend-why-its-more-than-just-a-meme/) is one of particular relevance during the Trump presidency. This trend first emerged in 2025 on TikTok, and still circulates prominently on short-form content platforms like Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts.

The trend shows individuals wearing Chinese clothing, using phrases such as "Becoming Chinese," showing Chinese cities such as Chongqing as futuristic cyberpunk cities, and generally viewing China as an exciting and almost mythical place. This trend is similar to the idolization that has been seen for Japan and other places considered exotic, but has a twist in that it presents a stereotypical American's view of China being authoritarian and hostile, and says that it's untrue. China is actually flourishing and more advanced than the U.S.

The trend reveals that both U.S. and global views on China are changing to become more favorable. According to the [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/04/14/americans-views-of-china-have-grown-somewhat-more-positive-in-recent-years/), the percentage of Americans with a favorable view of China was 27% as of April 2026, double what it was 3 years ago. Democrats and young people lead this charge, though the uptick is consistent across voter lines.

There are many possible explanations for this. The Trump presidency has tried to renegotiate the U.S.'s role in the global order and has alienated many allies through tariffs and selective giving of aid. Trump TACOs (Trump Always Chickens Out) have lowered U.S. citizens' confidence in his ability to make the right choices around policy, which has cemented China as a global rival opposite the United States.

While confidence among U.S. citizens in Xi Jinping making the right calls in global affairs is still low, it has still grown 8% since 2024. There are [high levels of dissatisfaction](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/poll-americans-anxious-pessimistic-deeply-unhappy-trump-ahead-midterms-rcna603064) over current politics in the United States. With a war in Iran, border security tensions running high, corruption seen as a higher risk, and growing inflation, it is almost natural to say the grass is greener on the other side and idealize the United States' biggest rival.

## Takeaways

Now, in the second term of a president suffering low popularity ratings, may be an opportune time for China to take charge of the world order. The success of Donald Trump has proven that perception, more so than substance, is the key to success. If China is able to convince the populace of Western countries and the Global South that they are a better long-term partner, then we may see countries begin to attract China not only in trade but ideologically as well.

According to research from the [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2026/07/15/people-in-many-countries-now-view-china-more-positively-than-the-u-s/), this strategy has been working, mainly because as perceptions of China have grown more popular, views of the U.S. have become increasingly negative.

This perception shift is a prerequisite to any physical, boots-on-the-ground activity that China may take in opposition to states such as Taiwan. China's grand strategy usually plays out very patiently, and to succeed in an operation against Taiwan, having support from the international community, or at least eroding your enemy's, would be extremely important to ensure acceptance for radical action or warfare.

Global actors such as China actively use social media to push their agendas on the user bases of these platforms. This is an apparent part of many global actors' foreign policy strategy to reshape elections, increase support for political candidates, or create stumbling blocks against certain unfavorable policies.

This also opens up further pathways to war and other usually unfavorable actions as global support for the nation as a whole increases. Both policymakers and individuals should be aware of the political undercurrents to their everyday, innocuous actions.
